Geomatics in Mojo

Geomatics is grid arithmetic wearing boots. A coordinate transformation is applied to millions of points, a least-squares adjustment solves a normal-equation system once per epoch, and a raster is one loop over cells. This tutorial works through the actual geodesy - the ellipsoid, ECEF, traverses, the transverse Mercator, GNSS trilateration and network adjustment - with a live widget on each idea and a quiz at the end of every module.

01 - Why Mojo for geomatics

The Earth is not a sphere, and every geomatics computation is a loop over that fact.

A geodetic position is a triple - latitude, longitude, height - and the surface it lives on is an ellipsoid, flattened so that the polar radius is about 21 km shorter than the equatorial one. Almost nothing in geomatics is closed-form on a sphere; the radius of curvature depends on where you are, so a transformation is a per-point evaluation of functions of latitude.

That shape of work - fixed fields, tight loops, no dynamic dispatch - is what a systems language is for. A struct holds a point; a loop walks a grid; the compiler keeps the fields in registers. Mojo gives you the loop and the struct, and the same source runs scalar or vectorised.

$$M(\varphi)=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{(1-e^2\sin^2\varphi)^{3/2}}, \qquad N(\varphi)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}}$$

$M$ is the meridian radius of curvature and $N$ the prime-vertical radius. They differ by roughly 1 km mid-latitude, and mixing them up is the classic source of a small, consistent distance error.

phi           = 0.898457101683137
M (meridian)  = 6374580.03779663
N (prime vert)= 6391244.876271445
mean radius   = 6382907.01835378

The WGS84 ellipsoid is fixed by two numbers: a semi-major axis $a = 6378137$ m and a flattening $f = 1/298.257223563$. Everything else - the square of eccentricity $e^2 = f(2-f)$, the polar radius, the radii above - is derived. Carrying those two constants as comptime values means they fold into the arithmetic.

Lab - the radii of curvaturem01

The widget plots the meridian radius (blue), the prime-vertical radius (green) and their geometric mean against latitude. The readouts give all three at the chosen latitude, plus the local flattening ratio $N/M$, which is the correction a surveyor applies to convert between the two.

Why the mean radius is 6 371 km

A sphere of radius $\sqrt{MN}$ matches the ellipsoid to second order at that latitude. It is not a global best-fit sphere - that is a different, smaller number - but it is the natural local replacement, and it is exact enough for order-of-magnitude work.

02 - Coordinate frames

Geodetic, Earth-centred Cartesian, and local tangent planes - and how to move between them.

Coordinates are not absolute; they are a description relative to a frame. Geodetic coordinates $(\varphi,\lambda,h)$ are intuitive and good for storage. Earth-centred Earth-fixed (ECEF) Cartesian $(X,Y,Z)$ are linear, so distances and differentiable operations live there. The conversion between them is the geodetic datum calculation.

Forward is closed. With $a$, $e^2$ and the prime-vertical radius $N(\varphi)$:

$$X=(N+h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\quad Y=(N+h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\quad Z=\bigl(N(1-e^2)+h\bigr)\sin\varphi$$ 
X = 3980609.237097504
Y = -104.2121061577852
Z = 4966859.728504293
lat = 0.898457101683137
lon = -0.000026179938779914945
h   = 45.0

The inverse is the awkward direction. Longitude is a simple atan2(y, x), but latitude appears on both sides of the equation because $N$ depends on it. The fix is a fixed-point iteration: start from a spherical estimate, then recompute $N$ and refine latitude until it stops moving. Hero of Alexandria had the same problem in two dimensions; here it takes about five passes to reach machine precision.

Height at the poles

The tidy inverse height $h = p/\cos\varphi - N$ divides by $\cos\varphi$, which vanishes at the poles. A robust routine switches to the $Z$-difference form $h = Z/\sin\varphi - N(1-e^2)$ inside a few degrees of the pole. Ignore this and the height blows up exactly where polar work happens.

Lab - geodetic, ECEF, and backm02

Drag the latitude, longitude and height sliders and the widget runs the same forward map and the same iteration as the listing. The right-hand readouts are the ECEF triple and the recovered geodetic triple; the round-trip residual shows how far the iteration landed from where it started. The baseline readout is the straight-line chord to a fixed reference point at Greenwich - the Euclidean distance in the ECEF frame, before the curvature is put back.

Why does ECEF latitude need iteration to invert, when longitude does not?

Why: Longitude is a pure rotation about the $Z$ axis, so it is a single atan2. Latitude enters the height and the ellipsoid normal, and $N(\varphi)$ is coupled to $\varphi$ - a small circular dependency that iteration resolves.

03 - Bearings and traverses

A chain of measured vectors should return to where it started - and the amount by which it misses is the whole art of surveying.

A traverse walks from one station to the next, turning a measured azimuth and distance into a coordinate increment. On a local plane the increment is a rotation of the distance by the bearing:

$$\Delta E = d\sin\alpha, \qquad \Delta N = d\cos\alpha$$

Sum those over a closed loop and the answer should be zero. It never is. The residual vector is the misclosure, and its length divided by the perimeter is the accuracy of the traverse, usually quoted as a ratio such as 1:5000 or, in the field, in parts per thousand.

sum E     = -0.20590782899220983
sum N     = -2.9476950868273804
perimeter = 400.3
misclose  = 2.954878061603653
accuracy  = 7.381658909826763 per mille

The example loop is a nominal square with one bearing two degrees out. The direction of the misclosure vector tells you which leg is suspect: a single gross error in one leg leaves a residual mostly along that leg's bearing-reversed direction, which is why a misclosure plot is diagnostic, not just a pass/fail number.

Lab - traverse closurem03

Edit the leg table - one azimuth, distance pair per line, azimuth in degrees - and the widget accumulates the loop exactly as the listing does. It draws the traverse, the closing vector from the last station back to the first, and prints the perimeter, misclosure and accuracy. Load the default and you will reproduce the listing's numbers to the last digit.

Field practice

A misclosure is never left in the raw coordinates. It is distributed back across the legs - by Bowditch (proportional to length) or by least squares - before anything is plotted or staked. Module 08's capstone carries the same loop through that adjustment step.

A closed traverse has a misclosure of 0.030 m over a perimeter of 600 m. What is the accuracy in parts per thousand?

Why: $(0.030/600)\times 1000 = 0.05$ parts per thousand, or a ratio of 1:20000. Small misclosures quoted as ratios are why a positive error ratio - more than one part - always means a blunder, not noise.

04 - Map projections

The transverse Mercator, the Krueger series, and the two numbers that make a projection usable: scale and convergence.

A projection trades a curved surface for a flat one. The transverse Mercator is conformal - it preserves angles - which makes it the workhorse of national grids. Points are not reproduced at true scale: the scale factor $k$ varies with position, chosen to equal $k_0$ on the central meridian and to grow or shrink away from it.

Karney's formulation writes the mapping as a sixth-order series in the third flattening $n = f/(2-f)$, evaluated on isometric coordinates. The series converges to sub-millimetre accuracy at six terms for a zone a few degrees wide.

$$n=\frac{f}{2-f},\qquad \xi'=\tan^{-1}\!\frac{\sinh\tau}{\cos\lambda},\qquad \eta'=\tanh^{-1}\!\frac{\sin\lambda}{\sqrt{1+\sinh^2\tau}}$$ 
Easting  = 583293.9244041431
Northing = 5706111.842722682

The widget runs the forward map, the inverse, and the local Jacobian to recover scale and convergence. A round trip should return to the input to within a hundredth of a millimetre; if it does not, the series order or the reduction to the footpoint is wrong.

Lab - forward, inverse, scale, convergencem04

Set a latitude and a longitude offset from the central meridian and the widget projects the point, inverts the result, and differentiates the map numerically to get the scale factor and the grid convergence. The two scale columns - from the north and the east derivatives of the Jacobian - should agree: that agreement is conformality, and it is the sharpest check the widget can make.

Convergence is not a bug

The angle between grid north and true north grows with longitude and latitude. A bearing measured on the ground must be rotated by $\gamma$ to plot on the grid, and failing to do so bends every long line.

On the projection's central meridian, what is the local scale factor?

Why: The transverse Mercator is a secant projection: $k = k_0 < 1$ on the central meridian and rises to 1 on two standard lines either side, then above 1 toward the zone edge. Choosing $k_0 < 1$ balances the maximum error across the zone.

05 - GNSS positioning

A fix is a least-squares problem: ranges to satellites, linearised around a guess, solved for position.

A satellite broadcasts its position and a time of flight, which gives a range. The receiver's position is the point whose distances to the satellites best match those ranges. With four or more satellites the system is over-determined and is solved by least squares.

The range is nonlinear in the position, so each iteration linearises about the current estimate. Writing the unit vector from the estimate to satellite $i$ as $(u_x,u_y)$, the design matrix rows are those unit vectors and the residual is the measured minus computed range. The normal equations solve for the correction $(\delta x,\delta y)$:

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}u_{x,1}&u_{y,1}\\ \vdots&\vdots\\ u_{x,n}&u_{y,n}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \delta = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top r$$ 
x    = 1200.5099142930003
y    = -799.9030216461338
HDOP = 1.0019400656430641

Two iterations of the display here would suffice; twelve are shown because a real receiver iterates until the correction is below a threshold, and it matters when the initial guess is far out. The matrix $(A^\top A)^{-1}$ is the covariance of the estimate scaled by the range variance, and its trace is the dilution of precision - the geometry penalty, independent of how good the ranges are.

Lab - position from rangesm05

The widget places a set of satellites on a sky plot and solves for the receiver position with the same linearised least squares as the listing. Move a satellite and the HDOP readout changes even though the ranges have not: that is geometry dilution, and the residual bars show which satellite pulls hardest against the fit.

DOP is geometry, not accuracy

A high DOP multiplies whatever range error you have. Satellites clustered in one part of the sky give a strong solution along the line to the cluster and a weak one across it, and no amount of averaging fixes that.

What does a small HDOP tell you about a fix?

Why: HDOP is $\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}[(A^\top A)^{-1}]}$: a pure function of the design matrix, hence of geometry. It scales the range measurement noise into position noise; it says nothing about how good the measured ranges were.

06 - Least-squares adjustment

Weights, normal equations, residuals and the a posteriori standard error that judges the whole adjustment.

Adjustment is least squares applied to measurements that carry different uncertainties. Each observation gets a weight $w$ - inversely proportional to its variance - and the normal equations stack as $A^\top W A\,x = A^\top W\,y$. For a straight line the two parameters are intercept and slope, and the closed form is a weighted linear fit.

$$\begin{bmatrix}\sum w & \sum wx\\ \sum wx & \sum wx^2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a\\ b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum wy\\ \sum wxy\end{bmatrix}$$ 
intercept a = 0.10454545454545454
slope     b = 1.9627272727272747
dof         = 3
s0          = 0.1764120520860526

Once the parameters are solved, the residuals $r_i = y_i - (a + bx_i)$ are the evidence of misfit. The a posteriori standard error $s_0 = \sqrt{\sum w_i r_i^2 / \text{dof}}$ estimates the standard deviation of an observation of unit weight. If it is far from 1, either the weights were optimistic or the model is wrong - and the residual pattern says which.

Lab - weighted fit and residualsm06

Slide one observation's weight down and watch the fitted line rotate toward the well-measured points. The residual bars should shrink at the heavy points and grow at the light one; the readout $\sigma_0$ is the same quantity the listing prints. When you de-weight an outlier the residuals concentrate on it, which is how a blunder is localised.

Degrees of freedom

With $n$ observations and $u$ parameters, the redundancy is $n-u$. Here $5-2 = 3$. Without redundancy there are no residuals to judge, and least squares is just an exact solve wearing a costume.

You halve the weight of one observation. What happens to the fitted line?

Why: The weight enters the normal equations directly, so a lower weight pulls the solution less. Both parameters can shift; only when every weight is equal does w cancel out of the solution entirely.

07 - Areas and volumes

Shoelace for the boundary, quadrature for the profile, prismoid for the cut and fill.

Three different questions look like one. The area of a polygon has an exact formula: the shoelace, a half-sum of cross products around the boundary. The area under a profile is an integral, and its accuracy depends on the quadrature rule. The volume between two surfaces is a chain of cross-sections carried along a prismoid rule.

$$A_{\text{poly}}=\frac12\Bigl|\sum_i (x_i y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} y_i)\Bigr|,\qquad V=\frac{L}{6}\bigl(A_1 + 4A_m + A_2\bigr)$$

The trapezoid rule joins samples with straight lines and is exact for a linear profile. Simpson's rule fits a parabola through each pair of intervals and is exact for a quadratic - so on the same samples it is closer to a smooth curve, at the cost of requiring an even number of intervals.

shoelace  = 10.0
trapezoid = 7.0
simpson   = 7.333333333333333
prismoid  = 608.3333333333334

The listing prints all four for fixed data so you can check each against a hand calculation. The polygon is exact at 10. On the same five-point profile, the trapezoid gives 7 and Simpson $7\tfrac13$; that gap is quadrature error, and on a real surface it is the difference between over- and under-ordering earthworks.

Lab - three methods, one profilem07

The widget evaluates a smooth profile and computes its area three ways: the exact integral, the trapezoid rule, and Simpson's rule over the same samples. Add samples and both rules converge to the exact answer; Simpson converges far faster. The readout shows the error of each, so the algebraic order of the rule is visible as a slope.

Shoelace sign

The raw shoelace sum is signed by the winding direction: counter-clockwise is positive, clockwise negative. Take the absolute value for an area, but keep the sign if you need to know the orientation - and never feed a polygon with a duplicated closing vertex.

On the same evenly-spaced samples of a smooth curve, which is generally more accurate?

Why: Trapezoid integrates linears exactly and Simpson quadratics, so for smooth data Simpson's error falls as $h^4$ against the trapezoid's $h^2$. The cost is that it needs an even interval count and a uniform step.

08 - Gridding and the capstone

A raster is one loop; a survey reduction is several, chained.

Most geomatics throughput is a single loop over a grid of cells, applying the same arithmetic to each. What matters is that the loop is vectorisable: independent iterations, no aliasing, and an accumulation the compiler can reorder. Unrolling by four exposes four independent chains, which a vectorised backend packs into one register.

$$S=\sum_{i=0}^{n^2-1}\sin^2 i$$ 
cells     = 1048576
scalar    = 524287.845245648
unrolled  = 524287.84524567844
rel diff  = 5.80646813268768e-14

The two sums are not bit-identical, and it would be dishonest to claim they are: reassociating the additions changes the rounding in the last place. The relative difference, one part in $10^{13}$, is the real answer - the arithmetic is correct, it just stopped being commutative in the bottom bits the moment the order changed.

Lab - grid size and unroll widthm08

The bench runs the scalar loop and an unrolled loop over the same grid and compares them. It reports the relative difference (which should sit near machine epsilon times the number of cells) and the ratio of the per-cell work, so the point of unrolling is a number you measure rather than a claim you accept.

Capstone - reduce a local survey

The capstone chains the whole tutorial. It closes a four-leg traverse, distributes the misclosure by Bowditch so each leg carries a share proportional to its length, applies the grid scale factor, and reports the adjusted station coordinates. The closing east and north are machine-level zero - the loop now closes because the error has been pushed back into the legs where it belongs.

Each column is cumulative distance, adjusted easting and adjusted northing, in metres. The pipeline is the same one every survey reduction software runs: measure, close, distribute, project, report. Change the Bowditch rule for a least-squares distribution and only the middle step changes - which is exactly why the modules are ordered the way they are.

After a correct Bowditch adjustment of a closed traverse, what is the closing error?

Why: Bowditch distributes exactly the observed misclosure across the legs, proportional to length. The sum of the corrections equals the negative of the misclosure, so the adjusted loop closes by construction - leaving only floating-point residue, as the readout shows.
