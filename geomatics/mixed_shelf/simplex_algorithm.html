00 Welcome to Operations Research

The philosophy before the arithmetic — a walk, blindfolded, up a diamond.

The simplex method — devised by George Dantzig in 1947 — is the crown jewel of linear programming, and its philosophy fits in one paragraph. Imagine you are blindfolded on the surface of a multi-dimensional diamond (a polytope), searching for its highest point. The method tells you to feel for an edge that slopes upward, walk along it to the next vertex, and then repeat. Because the feasible region is convex, the moment no edge still leads uphill you are guaranteed to be at the absolute peak — not a local high, the peak.

Everything on this page is scaffolding around that single idea: how the region is drawn on paper (standard form), how the feet find the edges (the tableau and the pivot rules), how the walking plays out slowly (the worked examples), what the machines actually run (Python and Mojo 1.x), and what the view from the peak reveals about the value of every resource (duality).

The magic of simplex is not just that it works, but that it only evaluates the vertices — the corner points — of the feasible region, completely ignoring the infinitely many points inside it. That is what makes it computationally efficient: a problem with thousands of constraints still visits a very small number of corners on the way to the answer.

01 What a linear program asks for

Before the algorithm, the shape of the question it answers.

A linear program asks for the best point in a region carved out by straight-line (or straight-hyperplane) constraints. "Best" means maximizing or minimizing a linear objective; "region" means every constraint is a linear inequality or equality. Written the way the simplex method expects — standard form — a maximization problem with n decision variables and m constraints looks like this:

Every ≤ constraint hides a piece of unused capacity — a slack variable s_i that soaks up the difference between what's available and what's used. Adding one slack per row turns every inequality into an equality:

This is the trick that makes the whole method work: the feasible region is a polytope — a solid with flat faces — and its corners (basic feasible solutions) are exactly the points where n of the n + m variables (structural plus slack) are pinned to zero. Simplex never wanders through the interior — it walks from corner to adjacent corner along the polytope's edges, and it only ever takes a step that doesn't make the objective worse.

Why corners are enough. If an LP has an optimal solution, one is attained at a corner of the polytope: a linear objective can't have a strictly-better interior point than every corner — you could always slide further in the direction of increase until you hit a face, then a corner. So the search space collapses from infinitely many points to a finite (if combinatorially large) set of vertices. This, plus boundedness, is the fundamental theorem of linear programming.

02 The tableau and the pivot rules

Five moves, repeated until no move improves the objective.

Set every slack variable as the starting basis (structural variables at zero — the origin is always feasible when every b_i ≥ 0) and arrange the whole system as a tableau: one row per constraint, one row for the objective, one column per variable, and a right-hand-side column. Then repeat:

  1. Check optimality. Look at the objective row. If every coefficient there is ≥ 0, no variable can enter and improve z further — stop, the current corner is optimal.
  2. Choose the entering variable. Otherwise, pick the column with the most negative coefficient in the objective row (Dantzig's rule). That variable increases z fastest per unit — it's about to leave zero and become part of the basis.
  3. Ratio test — choose the leaving variable. For every row with a positive coefficient in the entering column, divide its right-hand side by that coefficient. The row with the smallest ratio is the tightest constraint — it determines how far the entering variable can grow before some basic variable would go negative. That row's current basic variable leaves the basis.
  4. Pivot. Divide the leaving row by the pivot element so it reads 1 in the entering column, then subtract multiples of it from every other row (including the objective row) to zero that column everywhere else — the classic Gauss-Jordan elimination step.
  5. Repeat from step 1 on the new tableau.

Assembled once, the generic tableau looks like this — one identity block for the slacks, one column of right-hand sides, and the negated objective coefficients along the bottom:

Two things keep this from running forever. First, each pivot either strictly increases z or leaves it unchanged — it never decreases. Second, in the degenerate case where a tie in the ratio test could send the method in a circle, Bland's rule breaks ties by choosing the candidate with the smallest variable index, which provably prevents cycling. Both example solvers below use it as the tie-break.

06 When the origin is not feasible: Big-M and Two-Phase

Artificial variables, a penalty, or a fake objective — how real constraints get their start.

Both worked examples so far started happy: all constraints ≤ with b_i ≥ 0, so the origin is immediately feasible. Real problems bring ≥ and = constraints and negative right-hand sides. Two classical repairs — Big-M and Two-Phase — both add artificial variables to manufacture a starting basis, then get rid of them. Section 7.1 shows the loop, and section 7.2 is where all of it becomes a single HiGHS call.

First, normalize: any row with b_i < 0 gets multiplied through by −1 (flipping its sense). Then each row gets its columns:

row senseaddstarting basic variable
≤slack +1the slack
≥surplus −1 and artificial +1the artificial
=artificial +1the artificial

A small running example — solve it by hand both ways, then check it against HiGHS in section 7.2:

minimize  C = 2x₁ + 3x₂
subject to  x₁ + x₂ ≥ 4
              x₁, x₂ ≥ 0

As a maximization: maximize Z = −2x₁ − 3x₂. The single ≥ row becomes x₁ + x₂ − s₁ + a₁ = 4, with a₁ the artificial. (You can see the answer geometrically — push cost down until the line x₁ + x₂ = 4 — the point of the exercise is the machinery.)

Big-M — make artificials ruinously expensive

Penalize each artificial in the objective with a huge cost −M·a_i (for a max problem), so the optimizer drives artificials to zero on its own. The objective row starts as −c plus +M on artificial columns, then the basic artificials are priced out by subtracting M times their rows:

All coefficients ≥ 0 for large M — optimal: Z = −8, so min C = 8 at x = (4, 0) — exactly what HiGHS reports in section 7.2. The practical drawback of Big-M is numerical: "huge" M (often 10⁶ or more alongside coefficients of size 1) pollutes the ratio tests with round-off.

Two-Phase — solve a fake LP first

Phase I ignores the real objective and minimizes the sum of artificials (here: minimize w = a₁, i.e. maximize −a₁). If the minimum is 0, a feasible corner has been found; if it is positive, the LP is infeasible — a free infeasibility test Big-M doesn't give you cleanly. The Phase-I objective row is the negated sum of the artificial rows:

One fine point. If an artificial stays basic at value zero at the end of Phase I (a degenerate row), pivot it out on any nonzero non-artificial entry, or drop the row if it is entirely zero — a redundant constraint. HiGHS handles both cases for you (section 7.2).

07 Mojo implementations all outputs verified below

Two shapes of the same solver: the tableau loop flattened down to its essentials, and the one-line library call you would actually ship — SciPy's linprog, reached from Mojo.

The from-scratch solver — Dantzig's most-negative-coefficient rule for entering, the ratio test, Bland's tie-break for leaving — is transcribed to Mojo in section 9, where every step of the algorithm from section 2 maps onto a line of code. The two listings here are the shapes you actually meet in practice.

All of them assume the happy standard form of section 2: every constraint ≤, every b_i ≥ 0, so the all-slack origin is immediately a feasible corner. Section 6's toolkit is what lifts that restriction — flip negative RHS rows, add surplus and artificial columns, run Phase I to manufacture a feasible starting basis, then Phase II to optimize from there. Section 7.2 is where that entire sequence collapses into a single call.

7.1  simplex_tight.mojo — the from-scratch loop, with no bookkeeping

Once the algorithm is trusted, the label-tracking machinery of section 9 can go. In the Mojo version there is no basis array and no tie-break rule: each pivot is one row normalization plus one elimination sweep, and the solution is read straight off the right-hand-side column by recognising which column has become a unit vector. Same five steps, same numbers — a shorter program.

mojo 1.x — simplex_tight.mojo
# simplex_tight.mojo -- the same five-step method, flattened: each pivot
# updates the tableau with one row normalization plus one elimination sweep.
# Mojo 1.x -- same idioms as simplex.mojo, minus the basis bookkeeping.

def solve(c: List[Float64], A: List[List[Float64]], b: List[Float64]) raises -> Tuple[List[Float64], Float64, Int]:
    var m = len(A)                       # constraint rows
    var n = len(c)                       # structural variables
    var ncols = n + m + 1                # tableau columns, RHS included

    # ---- build the tableau: [ A | I | b ] ----
    var T = List[List[Float64]]()
    for i in range(m):
        var row = A[i].copy()
        for k in range(m):
            row.append(1.0 if k == i else 0.0)   # slack identity block
        row.append(b[i])                         # RHS
        T.append(row^)

    var obj: List[Float64] = [-v for v in c]    # negated objective
    for _ in range(m + 1):
        obj.append(0.0)                         # slack columns + RHS start at zero
    T.append(obj^)

    # ---- pivot loop: no basis array, no Bland tie-break ----
    var iteration = 0
    while True:
        var col = -1                     # entering column: most negative reduced cost
        var best = -1e-10
        for j in range(n + m):
            if T[m][j] < best:
                best = T[m][j]
                col = j
        if col == -1:
            break                        # optimal

        var row = -1                     # leaving row: strict minimum ratio
        var best_ratio = 1e30
        for i in range(m):
            if T[i][col] > 1e-10:
                var ratio = T[i][ncols - 1] / T[i][col]
                if ratio < best_ratio:
                    best_ratio = ratio
                    row = i
        if row == -1:
            raise Error("Problem is unbounded")

        var pivot = T[row][col]          # normalize the pivot row...
        for k in range(ncols):
            T[row][k] = T[row][k] / pivot
        for i in range(m + 1):           # ...and Gauss-Jordan the column
            if i != row:
                var factor = T[i][col]
                if abs(factor) > 1e-12:
                    for k in range(ncols):
                        T[i][k] = T[i][k] - factor * T[row][k]
        iteration += 1

    # ---- read the solution with no labels: column j belongs to row i when
    # ---- column j of the constraint block is the unit vector e_i
    var solution: List[Float64] = [0.0 for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(n):
            var is_basic = true
            for r in range(m):
                var want = 1.0 if r == i else 0.0
                if abs(T[r][j] - want) > 1e-9:
                    is_basic = false
            if is_basic:
                solution[j] = T[i][ncols - 1]

    return (solution^, T[m][ncols - 1], iteration)   # x, Z, pivots


def main() raises:
    var c1: List[Float64] = [3.0, 5.0]
    var A1: List[List[Float64]] = [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 2.0], [3.0, 2.0]]
    var b1: List[Float64] = [4.0, 12.0, 18.0]
    var (x1, z1, k1) = solve(c1, A1, b1)
    print("Example A: x =", x1[0], x1[1], " Z =", z1, " pivots =", k1)

    var c2: List[Float64] = [5.0, 4.0, 3.0]
    var A2: List[List[Float64]] = [[2.0, 3.0, 1.0], [4.0, 1.0, 2.0], [3.0, 4.0, 2.0]]
    var b2: List[Float64] = [5.0, 11.0, 8.0]
    var (x2, z2, k2) = solve(c2, A2, b2)
    print("Example B: x =", x2[0], x2[1], x2[2], " Z =", z2, " pivots =", k2)

    # Example C -- two coupled capacity constraints
    var c3: List[Float64] = [3.0, 2.0]
    var A3: List[List[Float64]] = [[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 3.0]]
    var b3: List[Float64] = [4.0, 6.0]
    var (x3, z3, k3) = solve(c3, A3, b3)
    print("Example C: x =", x3[0], x3[1], " Z =", z3, " pivots =", k3)

Note the two simplifications versus simplex.mojo in section 9: no Bland tie-break (the scan keeps the first strict minimum) and no exact-fraction arithmetic — fine for textbook sizes. The cost of dropping the basis array is a cubic unit-vector test at the end instead of a linear lookup, which is invisible at this size and irrelevant at any real one. Example C is a reminder to always check a claimed optimum: the "obvious" corner (3, 1) with Z = 11 is not optimal — the edge keeps improving to (4, 0) with Z = 12, which the solver finds in one pivot.

7.2  linprog_demo.mojo — what you'd actually ship

For real workloads you don't maintain a tableau at all — you call a solver. SciPy's linprog wraps the HiGHS library (a modern dual simplex / interior-point implementation in C++), and Mojo reaches it through the interop bridge. Two caveats trip people up: it minimizes by default, so a maximization problem needs a negated objective, and ≥ constraints must be multiplied through by −1 to become ≤. The point of the listing below is the shape: Mojo owns the program — the objective, the constraints, the printing — and SciPy is called as a library from inside a compiled def.

mojo 1.x — linprog_demo.mojo
# linprog_demo.mojo -- Mojo driving SciPy's linprog (HiGHS) through interop.
# Mojo 1.x: def functions, `var` bindings, `from std.python import Python`.

from std.python import Python

def main():
    var optimize = Python.import_module("scipy.optimize")

    # --- Maximization: Example A (Wyndor Glass) -----------------------
    # linprog minimizes, so maximize Z = 3x1 + 5x2  becomes  minimize -Z
    var res = optimize.linprog(
        c=[-3, -5],                 # negated objective
        A_ub=[[1, 0],               # x1 <= 4
              [0, 2],               # 2x2 <= 12
              [3, 2]],              # 3x1 + 2x2 <= 18
        b_ub=[4, 12, 18],
        bounds=[(0, None), (0, None)],
        method="highs")
    print("max Z = ", -res.fun, "  at  x1 = ", res.x[0], ", x2 = ", res.x[1])

    # --- Minimization with a >= constraint ----------------------------
    # minimize C = 2x1 + 3x2  s.t.  x1 + x2 >= 4,  x >= 0
    # x1 + x2 >= 4   becomes   -x1 - x2 <= -4
    var res_min = optimize.linprog(
        c=[2, 3],
        A_ub=[[-1, -1]],
        b_ub=[-4],
        bounds=[(0, None), (0, None)],
        method="highs")
    print("min C = ", res_min.fun, "  at  x1 = ", res_min.x[0], ", x2 = ", res_min.x[1])

09 The same solver in Mojo 1.x

Same algorithm, different guarantees — mostly about who owns what memory.

Mojo 1.x (shipped with Modular's 26.5 release) offers Python-shaped syntax with compile-time typing and ownership — attractive for the simplex inner loop, which is dense linear algebra on a mutable 2-D structure. Four language notes, all visible in the listing:

  • def is the one function keyword. A def is fully compiled when annotated; raises marks code that can raise.
  • var declares a binding — required at function scope in Mojo, and the only kind of local binding.
  • A List owns its elements — it is not implicitly copyable. Appending a locally built list into a list-of-lists requires the transfer operator ^ (you give ownership away), and duplicating an existing list requires an explicit .copy(). A plain var row = A[i] won't compile.
  • List literals ([3.0, 5.0]) and comprehensions ([-v for v in c]) build new owned values — they cut boilerplate without relaxing ownership.

Same solver contract as simplex.mojo in section 9: returns the solution vector, the optimal value, and the pivot count.

mojo 1.x — simplex.mojo
# simplex.mojo -- tableau-form primal simplex for standard-form LPs:
#   maximize c^T x  subject to  A x <= b,  x >= 0,  b >= 0
# Mojo 1.x (Modular 26.5) -- def functions, `var` bindings, List[Float64].

def solve(c: List[Float64], A: List[List[Float64]], b: List[Float64]) raises -> Tuple[List[Float64], Float64, Int]:
    var m = len(A)                       # constraint rows
    var n = len(c)                       # structural variables
    var ncols = n + m + 1                # tableau columns, RHS included

    # ---- build the tableau: [ A | I | b ], one row at a time ----
    var T = List[List[Float64]]()
    for i in range(m):
        var row = A[i].copy()            # List isn't implicitly copyable
        for k in range(m):
            row.append(1.0 if k == i else 0.0)   # slack identity block
        row.append(b[i])                 # RHS
        T.append(row^)                   # ^ hands ownership of row to T

    var obj: List[Float64] = [-v for v in c]     # comprehension: negated objective
    for _ in range(m + 1):
        obj.append(0.0)                  # slack columns + RHS start at zero
    T.append(obj^)

    var basis = List[Int]()
    for i in range(m):
        basis.append(n + i)              # slacks are the starting basis

    # ---- pivot loop: Dantzig entering, ratio test + Bland tie-break leaving ----
    var iteration = 0
    while True:
        var col = -1                     # entering column: most negative
        var best = -1e-10                # ... objective-row coefficient
        for j in range(n + m):
            if T[m][j] < best:
                best = T[m][j]
                col = j
        if col == -1:
            break                        # optimal: no negative reduced cost remains

        var row = -1                     # leaving row: minimum ratio
        var best_ratio = 1e30
        for i in range(m):
            if T[i][col] > 1e-10:        # only positive entries can block
                var ratio = T[i][ncols - 1] / T[i][col]
                if ratio < best_ratio - 1e-12 or (
                    ratio < best_ratio + 1e-12 and (row == -1 or basis[i] < basis[row])
                ):
                    best_ratio = ratio   # strict improvement, or a tie won
                    row = i              # ... by the smaller basis index (Bland)
        if row == -1:
            raise Error("Problem is unbounded")

        var pivot = T[row][col]          # normalize the pivot row...
        for k in range(ncols):
            T[row][k] = T[row][k] / pivot
        for i in range(m + 1):           # ...and Gauss-Jordan the column
            if i != row:
                var factor = T[i][col]
                if abs(factor) > 1e-12:
                    for k in range(ncols):
                        T[i][k] = T[i][k] - factor * T[row][k]
        basis[row] = col
        iteration += 1

    # ---- read the solution off the final tableau ----
    var solution: List[Float64] = [0.0 for _ in range(n)]   # nonbasic vars stay 0
    for i in range(m):
        if basis[i] < n:                 # a structural variable in the basis
            solution[basis[i]] = T[i][ncols - 1]    # ... takes the row's RHS

    return (solution^, T[m][ncols - 1], iteration)  # x, Z, pivots

Two ownership details survive even with literals, and both come straight from the language rules: indexing a List returns a reference, so building a tableau row from A[i] needs an explicit .copy() — and appending any locally built list into the tableau still needs the ^ transfer. The literals only remove boilerplate; they don't relax ownership. Everything else about the algorithm is unchanged from the Python version: same entering rule, same ratio test, same Bland tie-break, same pivot arithmetic. The only genuinely new ideas in the Mojo column are about ownership, not about linear programming.

Why bother? The whole simplex inner loop is dense linear algebra on a mutable 2-D array — exactly the workload Mojo's ownership model and MLIR-based compilation are built for. The Python version is completely adequate for textbook-sized examples; on a tableau with thousands of variables and constraints, the statically-typed, bounds-checked-once Mojo version compiles down much closer to what a hand-written C loop would look like, without giving up the readable, Python-shaped syntax.

10 Duality — prices hiding in the final tableau

One LP, two readings: the primal allocates, the dual prices — and they meet at the same number.

Every problem on this page has been read one way: as an allocation question — which products, at what levels, earn the most inside the capacities. The same data supports a second, inverted reading: a pricing question — what is each unit of each capacity worth, if the prices must exactly explain the profit? The two readings are the primal and the dual:

The correspondence is total. The m primal constraints become m dual variables y_i; the n primal variables become n dual constraints; the objective coefficients c trade places with the right-hand sides b; max becomes min; ≤ becomes ≥. Neither problem is primary — the dual of the dual is the primal again.

Weak duality in one line. For any primal-feasible x and dual-feasible y: c^T x ≤ b^T y — the first step because A^T y ≥ c and x ≥ 0, the second because Ax ≤ b and y ≥ 0. So every dual-feasible price vector upper-bounds every feasible plan: for Wyndor, y = (0, 2, 1) is dual-feasible, certifying Z ≤ 42 before a single pivot. At the optimum the bound closes completely: z* = w* — strong duality.
The worked example of section 3 certifies all of this by hand. Its dual has two variables and two rows: min 6y₁ + 8y₂ subject to y₁ + 2y₂ ≥ 4, y₁ + y₂ ≥ 3, y ≥ 0. The price vector y = (3, 1) is dual-feasible, certifying bᵀy = 26 as an upper bound on Z before a single pivot — weak duality, again. And the final objective row of that section, under the slacks, reads exactly y* = (2, 1): both dual rows are tight (2 + 2 = 4, 2 + 1 = 3), both primal constraints bind, and bᵀy = 20 = Z*.

Complementary slackness — the audit rule

At the optimum, primal and dual tighten against each other pairwise:

In words: a constraint with slack carries a zero price (nobody pays for surplus), and a product run at a positive level sits under a tight dual constraint (its price exactly explains its profit). Wyndor's audit: s₁ = 2 > 0 forces y₁ = 0; x₁, x₂ > 0 force both dual constraints tight — 3 = 3 and 5 = 5. Example B shows the other side: x₂ = 0 and its dual column is slack by exactly 3 — the same 3 that sits in x₂'s column of that final tableau. This is why duality is the standard optimality certificate: produce a primal plan, a dual price vector, equality c^T x = b^T y, and the slack conditions — and no separate proof of optimality is needed.

Two relatives worth knowing

The dual simplex method keeps the objective row optimal-looking (dual-feasible: all coefficients ≥ 0) while the right-hand column still holds negatives (primal-infeasible), each iteration picking the leaving row by the most negative RHS and the entering column by a ratio test over the objective row — every primal rule from section 2, applied to the mirrored problem. It shines exactly where re-solving happens: add a cut or tighten a constraint, and the previous basis stays dual-feasible, so the dual simplex repairs it in a few pivots instead of restarting from Phase I. This is one half of what HiGHS does inside SciPy's linprog.

The revised simplex method is what production codes actually implement: instead of maintaining and pivoting the full m × (n+m) tableau, it keeps only the basis inverse (or its LU factors) and computes just the entering column's prices and the RHS on demand. Same pivots, same answers — far less arithmetic per iteration when n ≫ m, and it enables the sparse-matrix handling where real LPs (millions of columns) live.

11 Degeneracy, cycling, and where simplex struggles

The method is not perfect — three honest caveats.

Degeneracy and cycling

When two or more basic variables tie for the minimum ratio, the next tableau can have a basic variable sitting at zero — a degenerate vertex. Left unguarded, a sequence of degenerate pivots can cycle back to a tableau seen before and loop forever (Beale's 1955 example demonstrates it in six pivots). Bland's rule — always break ties, both for entering and leaving, by the smallest variable index — provably eliminates cycling, at the cost of sometimes being slower per iteration than more aggressive pivoting rules. Both implementations on this page use it as the tie-break specifically for this reason.

Worst-case exponential, typical-case fast

The Klee–Minty cube (1972) is a family of otherwise ordinary-looking LPs, one per dimension, constructed so that Dantzig's rule visits every single vertex — 2ⁿ of them — before finding the optimum. So in the worst case, simplex is exponential. In practice, on real-world problems, it reliably takes a low-degree-polynomial number of pivots in the number of constraints — which is why it remained the default LP method for decades despite the theoretical blemish, and why interior-point methods (which are polynomial-time in the worst case) only displaced it for the very largest problems.

Getting from "any LP" to standard form

Section 5's toolkit — flip negative RHS rows, add slacks/surpluses/artificials, run Phase I — covers ≥, =, and negative right-hand sides. Free (unsigned) variables need one more trick: split x = x⁺ − x⁻ with x⁺, x⁻ ≥ 0. After that, Phase I either finds a feasible corner or proves there isn't one, and Phase II optimizes from there — exactly what HiGHS absorbs for you in section 7.2.
