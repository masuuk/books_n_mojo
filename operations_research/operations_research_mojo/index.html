Operations Research in Mojo

Operations research turns a decision into a model: variables, an objective, and constraints. Solving it is arithmetic over that model - evaluate the objective, test feasibility, sweep the neighbours of a simplex, relax an integer, settle a network. This tutorial works through the real methods - LP geometry, the simplex method, duality, branch-and-bound thinking, Dijkstra, M/M/c queues, gradient descent and Amdahl's law - with a live widget on each idea and a quiz at the end of every module.

01 - Why Mojo for optimisers

A model is a struct; solving it is a loop over that struct, millions of times.

An optimisation model has three parts: decision variables, an objective to maximise or minimise, and constraints the variables must respect. Once the model is fixed, the work is arithmetic - multiply a coefficient by a variable, add, compare against a bound. That shape - fixed fields, tight loops, no dynamic dispatch - is exactly what a systems language is built for.

Mojo gives you a struct to hold the plan and the coefficients, a loop to evaluate it, and the option to run the same source scalar or vectorised. The first listing evaluates a two-product plan: its profit and the four resources it consumes.

$$\max\; z=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf{b},\;\mathbf{x}\ge 0$$

The objective is a dot product; each constraint row is another. Everything later in this tutorial - pivots, duals, relaxations - is bookkeeping on top of that dot product, and it stays cheap because the data lives in contiguous fields.

z     = 19.0
labor = 22.0
steel = 5.0
wood  = 3.0
paint = 1.0

The plan makes $3$ of the first product and $1$ of the second. Profit is $5\cdot3+4\cdot1=19$, and the labor row costs $6\cdot3+4\cdot1=22$ of its available $24$. Nothing here needed a solver - but every solver is this loop wrapped in a search.

Lab - profit and resource usagem01

The widget evaluates a plan $(x,y)$ and plots each resource against its capacity. Push a variable until a bar fills and that resource binds; the binding rows are the constraints that shape the optimum you will meet in module 02.

Why a struct, not separate arrays

A Plan keeps its fields adjacent, so the objective and the usage loop read the same two numbers repeatedly. When the model is a few hundred variables, that locality is the difference between fitting in cache and not.

A constraint is called binding at a point when

Why: A constraint binds when slack is zero, i.e. the resource is fully consumed. In the listing, the labor row reaches $22$ against a limit of $24$, so labor is not yet binding; increase $x$ and it will bind.

02 - Linear programming and its geometry

The feasible set is a polygon, and the optimum is always at a corner.

A linear program with two variables can be drawn. Each constraint is a half-plane; their intersection is a convex polygon - the feasible set. Because both the objective and the constraints are linear, the objective's contours are parallel lines, and sliding such a line against a convex polygon last touches it at a vertex. So you never need to search the interior: check the corners.

$$\max\; z=5x+4y\quad\text{s.t.}\quad 6x+4y\le 24,\;\; x+2y\le 6,\;\; x,y\ge 0$$

The four vertices come from intersecting pairs of the four boundary lines (two constraints plus the two axes). The listing enumerates every intersection and keeps only the feasible ones.

vertex 3.0 1.5 -> z = 21.0
vertex 4.0 0.0 -> z = 20.0
vertex 0.0 3.0 -> z = 12.0
vertex 0.0 0.0 -> z = 0.0
best x = 3.0
best y = 1.5
best z = 21.0

The optimum is $(3,1.5)$ with $z=21$: both resources fully used. The corner $(4,0)$ gives $20$ and is the runner-up - the fact that it is on the axis is a warning for module 05, where whole units are required.

Lab - the feasible polygonm02

The widget draws the feasible polygon (shaded), the objective direction, and the optimal vertex. Move the two coefficients or the right-hand sides and watch the optimum jump from one corner to another.

Why corners, not the whole polygon

If a point is optimal, so is any point on the segment between optimal points - a consequence of linearity. That means at least one optimal point is a vertex, so a finite enumeration always finds an optimum. The simplex method is just a clever way to visit only a few vertices instead of all of them.

For a bounded linear program, where does an optimal solution always lie?

Why: A linear objective over a convex polytope attains its optimum on the boundary, and if an edge is optimal, both its endpoints (vertices) are too. So a vertex always suffices - the basis of the simplex method.

03 - The simplex method

Walk from vertex to vertex, improving the objective at every step.

Enumerating every vertex is exponential. The simplex method visits only the ones that improve the objective. It keeps the constraint system in a tableau and repeatedly picks the column with the most negative reduced cost - the direction that improves $z$ fastest - then pivots until no improving direction remains.

$$\text{entering}: j=\arg\min_k \bar{c}_k,\qquad \text{leaving}: i=\arg\min_{a_{ik}>0}\frac{b_i}{a_{ik}}$$

The ratio test picks the leaving row so the new basic solution stays feasible. The listing starts from the slack basis, performs the two pivots this LP needs, and reports the optimal basis.

pivots = 2
x = 3.0
y = 1.5
z = 21.0

Two pivots take the slack solution $(0,0)$ to the optimum $(3,1.5)$: first $x$ enters and slack $s_1$ leaves, then $y$ enters and $s_2$ leaves. Each pivot is a Gauss-Jordan elimination on one column.

Lab - step through the pivotsm03

The widget shows the tableau at each iteration. Step forward to watch a pivot normalise its row and eliminate the entering column from the rest, and watch the current objective rise monotonically.

Degeneracy and cycling

If the ratio test ties, a pivot can leave a basic variable at zero - a degenerate step with no improvement. Bland's rule (choose the smallest index among tied candidates) guarantees the method terminates, at the cost of more pivots.

Which row leaves the basis in a simplex pivot?

Why: Growing the entering variable moves along an edge; the first constraint that blocks it (smallest $b_i/a_{ik}$ with $a_{ik}>0$) sets the largest feasible step. That row becomes nonbasic. If no ratio is positive, the problem is unbounded.

04 - Duality and sensitivity

Every LP has a shadow problem whose variables are the prices of the constraints.

The dual of a maximisation is a minimisation over the constraint prices. Each primal constraint becomes a dual variable - its shadow price: the rate the objective would rise if that resource limit increased by one unit. At the optimum the primal and dual objectives are equal (strong duality), so the dual certifies the primal's value.

$$\min\; 24y_1+6y_2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad 6y_1+y_2\ge 5,\;\; 4y_1+2y_2\ge 4,\;\; y_1,y_2\ge 0$$

Both primal constraints bind at the optimum, so both dual constraints hold with equality. That gives a $2\times2$ system, solved in the listing.

shadow price y1 = 0.75
shadow price y2 = 0.5
dual objective  = 21.0
allowable b1 min = 12.0
allowable b1 max = 36.0

The shadow prices are $0.75$ and $0.5$, and the dual objective matches the primal $z=21$. The last two lines are the allowable range on $b_1$: between $12$ and $36$ the same basis stays optimal, so raising the limit by one unit buys exactly $0.75$ of profit throughout that range.

Lab - shadow prices and rangesm04

The widget lets you perturb the first resource limit and recomputes the optimum. Inside the allowable range the profit moves along a straight line of slope $y_1$; outside it, the basis changes and the slope does too.

Complementary slackness

A constraint with slack has shadow price zero. In the listing, raising the limit on a non-binding resource changes nothing - there was spare capacity anyway. Prices live only on the binding rows.

A shadow price of $0.75$ on a resource limit means

Why: The shadow price is the derivative of the optimal objective with respect to that right-hand side. One more unit of a binding resource raises profit by $0.75$ - until the basis changes at the edge of the allowable range.

05 - Integer programming

When variables must be whole, the fractional optimum may be out of reach.

Real plans come in whole units. Requiring integer variables makes the problem harder: the LP optimum can be fractional, and simply rounding it can be infeasible or suboptimal - as the capstone will show. The smallest integer problem is the $0/1$ knapsack, where each item is either taken or not.

$$\max\sum_i v_i x_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_i w_i x_i\le C,\;\; x_i\in\{0,1\}$$

A dynamic program over capacity gives the exact integer optimum: dp[c] is the best value achievable with capacity $c$. The fractional relaxation - filling the highest value-per-weight items first, splitting the last - bounds it from above.

best value       = 8
fractional bound = 8.25
gap              = 0.25

With weights $\{2,3,4,5\}$, values $\{3,4,5,6\}$ and capacity $6$, the best whole plan takes items $1$ and $3$ for value $8$. The fractional relaxation allots one quarter of item $3$ and reaches $8.25$, so the integrality gap here is only $0.25$.

Lab - integer versus fractionalm05

The widget sweeps the capacity and plots the integer DP value against the fractional bound. Where the two curves separate, the gap is the price of integrality; where they touch, rounding is free.

Greedy is not always optimal

Taking items by value-per-weight is optimal for the fractional knapsack but can fail for the integer one: a high-ratio small item can block a slightly lower-ratio pair that weighs in better. The DP does not make that mistake.

Why can rounding an LP optimum fail for an integer program?

Why: Constraints can be tight at the fractional optimum, so rounding up violates them and rounding down loses value. The true integer optimum may sit at a different corner entirely - which is why branch and bound, not rounding, is the general tool.

06 - Networks

Routing, shortest paths, and flows are optimisation on a graph.

Many operational problems are defined on a network: a road map, a supply chain, a communication mesh. The shortest-path problem asks for the cheapest route from a source to every node. Dijkstra's algorithm settles nodes in order of distance: repeatedly take the nearest unsettled node, then relax the edges leaving it.

$$d(v)=\min_{u\to v}\bigl(d(u)+w(u,v)\bigr)$$

The listing runs Dijkstra on a six-node undirected graph from node $0$, using an $O(n^2)$ scan for the nearest unsettled node - the right choice when the graph is dense and edges are stored compactly.

dist to node 0 = 0.0
dist to node 1 = 7.0
dist to node 2 = 9.0
dist to node 3 = 20.0
dist to node 4 = 20.0
dist to node 5 = 11.0

The nearest node after the source is node $1$ at $7$, then node $2$ at $9$; node $5$ is reached cheaply via node $2$ ($9+2=11$), and nodes $3$ and $4$ both settle at $20$. Notice node $4$ is reached through node $5$, not the direct-looking route through node $3$ - order of settlement matters.

Lab - settle the networkm06

The widget draws the graph and lets you move the source. Settled nodes and the shortest-path tree update live, with each node labelled by its distance.

Negative weights break Dijkstra

Dijkstra assumes distances never decrease as you settle, so a negative edge can invalidate an already-settled node. For negative edges you need Bellman-Ford; for all-pairs you need Floyd-Warshall. Both are still just loops over the same adjacency data.

Dijkstra's algorithm settles the nearest unsettled node because

Why: With non-negative weights, any route to the nearest unsettled node goes through another unsettled node and is therefore at least as long. Its tentative distance is final, so it can be settled and never revisited - giving $O(n^2)$ (or $O(m\log n)$ with a heap).

07 - Queues

Capacity planning is queueing theory: how long, how many, how often full.

A service with random arrivals and random service times is a queue. The M/M/c model - Poisson arrivals, exponential service, $c$ servers - has closed-form steady-state results. Erlang-C gives the probability an arrival has to wait; the average queue length and wait follow from it.

$$L_q=\frac{P_0\,a^c\,\rho}{c!\,(1-\rho)^2},\qquad W_q=\frac{L_q}{\lambda},\qquad W=W_q+\frac{1}{\mu},\qquad L=\lambda W$$

Here $a=\lambda/\mu$ is the offered load and $\rho=a/c$ the utilisation; the system is stable only when $\rho<1$. The listing computes the full set for $\lambda=6$, $\mu=4$, $c=2$.

rho  = 0.75
P0   = 0.14285714285714285
Lq   = 1.9285714285714284
Wq   = 0.3214285714285714
W    = 0.5714285714285714
L    = 3.4285714285714284

At $75\%$ utilisation the average queue is about $1.93$ customers and a customer waits about $0.32$ time units before service. Queueing is nonlinear in $\rho$: push utilisation toward $1$ and $L_q$ blows up, which is why systems are provisioned to stay well below full.

Little's law ties it together

$L=\lambda W$ holds for any stable queue, not just M/M/c: the average number in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time in the system. It is a conservation identity, and a fast sanity check on any simulator.

08 - Nonlinear and multi-objective optimisation

When the objective curves, the optimum has no corner to sit on.

Real objectives are not always linear - cost curves bend, risk and return trade off. A smooth nonlinear objective has no vertices, so the geometric shortcut is gone. The workhorse is gradient descent: step in the direction of steepest decrease, $x\leftarrow x-\alpha\nabla f$.

$$f(x,y)=x^2+y^2+xy-6x-6y,\qquad \nabla f=(2x+y-6,\;x+2y-6)$$

This coupled quadratic is minimised at $(2,2)$, where the gradient vanishes. The listing runs $40$ fixed steps of size $\alpha=0.1$ from the origin.

x = 1.9999987266388477
y = 1.9999987266388477
f = -11.999999999995136

After $40$ steps the iterate is at $(1.9999987,1.9999987)$, essentially the minimum, with $f\approx-12$. The step size matters: too small and convergence crawls, too large and the iterates overshoot and diverge. For a quadratic, stability needs $\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}$.

Lab - the descent pathm08

The widget draws the iterates on the objective's contours. Shrink or grow the step and watch the path either settle smoothly, zig-zag, or spiral out.

Local, not global

Gradient descent finds a stationary point, which for a non-convex objective may be a local minimum or a saddle. Multi-objective problems make it worse: there is no single optimum, only a Pareto front of non-dominated trade-offs, and the method must be told how to trade them off.

Choosing too large a step size in gradient descent typically causes

Why: A step larger than about $2/\lambda_{\max}$ (for a quadratic) makes the error grow each iteration instead of shrinking, so the iterates diverge. Step-size selection - line search, momentum, or adaptive rules - is central in practice.

09 - Complexity, parallelism, and the capstone

How far does the work parallelise, and what does the whole pipeline produce?

Optimisation is often compute-bound - large LPs, Monte-Carlo simulation, dense linear algebra. Amdahl's law bounds the payoff from parallelism: if a fraction $p$ of the work parallelises perfectly across $n$ workers, the speedup is capped by the serial part no matter how many workers you add.

$$S(n)=\frac{1}{(1-p)+\dfrac{p}{n}}\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;\frac{1}{1-p}$$

With $p=0.95$ and $8$ cores the speedup is about $5.93$; the ceiling is $20$, reached only with infinitely many cores.

parallel fraction = 0.95
cores             = 8.0
speedup           = 5.925925925925925
ceiling           = 20.0
Lab - bounds on speedupm09

The widget plots speedup against core count for the chosen parallel fraction. Drag $p$ down and watch the curve flatten early - the serial fraction is the real limit.

Where the parallelism is

The loops in this tutorial are the parallel part: evaluating objectives, sweeping vertices, relaxing edges, simulating queues. Mojo's parallelize and vectorised kernels expose that work to the hardware; the serial bookkeeping (pivots, priority scans) is what Amdahl warns you about.

The capstone puts the pieces in order. Take the same production LP, solve it continuously, then decide what a factory can actually schedule. Naive rounding of the LP optimum is not the integer optimum - so enumerate the integer plans.

LP profit      = 21.0
rounded plan   = 19.0
integer plan   = 4 0
integer profit = 20.0
gap            = 1.0

The continuous optimum earns $21$, but it wants $1.5$ of the second product. Rounding to $(3,1)$ drops profit to $19$; the true best integer plan is $(4,0)$ at $20$. The integrality gap is $1$ - small here, but the lesson generalises: model, relax, then account for the units your operation actually ships in.

Lab - continuous versus integer plansm09

The widget plots the integer grid against the feasible polygon, marks the LP vertex and the best integer point, and reports the gap. It is the whole tutorial in one picture: geometry, optimum, integrality.

In the capstone, why is the best integer plan $(4,0)$ rather than the rounded $(3,1)$?

Why: Rounding the fractional coordinate down is only a heuristic. $(4,0)$ uses all of the first resource ($24$) and stays within the second ($4\le6$), for profit $5\cdot4=20$, which beats the rounded $19$. Enumerating - or branching - finds it; guessing does not.
