1. Orientation & Learning Objectives

Two ideas underpin almost every corporate-finance decision: net present value — whether a stream of future cash flows is worth more than it costs to obtain — and amortisation — how a fixed-payment loan is actually paid down, and what that ends up costing you. This book teaches both from first principles in Mojo 1.x, so every figure on the page is produced by compiled, typed code rather than by hand.

By the end of this book you will be able to:

  • Explain the time value of money and discount any future cash flow to its present value.
  • Compute the NPV of a cash-flow stream in Mojo 1.x — and apply the decision rule (accept when NPV > 0).
  • Sweep discount rates to build an NPV profile and read the internal rate of return where NPV crosses zero.
  • Derive the level-payment formula for a loan and generate an amortisation schedule month by month.
  • Quantify the interest a mortgage really costs, and model prepayment (extra principal) so you can judge how much it saves.
  • Combine the two: evaluate a loan-funded project end to end (payment → net inflow → NPV).
  • Port every one of these calculations to compiled Mojo 1.x — typed, native-speed financial code.

2. The Time Value of Money

Money has a time value: ₹100 today is worth more than ₹100 a year from now, because today’s rupees can be invested and earn a return while you wait. The discount rate r is the price of that waiting — your opportunity cost of capital, typically what a comparable-risk investment returns per period.

Compounding moves money forward. ₹100 at 10% per year becomes:

FV = PV · (1 + r)t   →   100 · 1.102 = 121

Discounting moves money backward — the inverse operation. ₹121 arriving in two years, at 10%, is worth exactly ₹100 of today’s money:

PV = FV / (1 + r)t   →   121 / 1.102 = 100
Why compound, not simple interest? A rupee earned at the end of year 1 is itself invested for year 2 and earns interest on interest. The exponent t in (1+r)t is what enforces “interest on interest”. Two formulae in this book are just this single rule applied twice: NPV discounts a stream of flows; the amortisation formula inverts it to solve for the payment that makes a stream sum to a known present value (the loan principal).

Intuition. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow solely because of what a dollar today can do in the meantime. Raise the discount rate and the future shrinks; lower it and the future looms larger. That one sentence explains why interest rates move the prices of everything.

Two practical conventions worth fixing now:

  • The exponent starts at t = 0, so cash flow today is never discounted (it is already in today’s money).
  • If the period is a month rather than a year, use the monthly rate rm = annual / 12 and count months, so the units always match.

3. Net Present Value

A project is a stream of cash flows: usually a negative outflow today (what it costs) followed by positive inflows. Net present value discounts every flow back to today and sums them:

NPV = Σt=0…n   CFt / (1 + r)t
  • CFt – cash flow at time t; CF0 is today (usually negative: the investment).
  • r – discount rate per period (opportunity cost of capital).
  • n – number of periods in the project’s life.

3.1 The decision rule

Accept when NPV > 0 — the project creates value after paying for capital. Reject when NPV < 0. At NPV = 0 you earn exactly the cost of capital — indifferent, and you may throw flakes: accept or reject, neither destroys value.

3.2 Worked example: a project that pays

You invest $1,000 today and expect $300, $400, $500, $600 over the next four years. Your discount rate is 10%. Should you invest? Discount each flow and add the undiscounted −1000:

The €500 arriving in year 3 is worth only 375.66 today; the ₹600 in year 4 just 409.81. Discounting prefers early money.

Mojo 1.x — one typed, compiled loop over the flows. Mojo’s ** powers take a Float64 exponent, so cast t (an Int) before raising:

from std.math import pow

def npv(rate: Float64, cash_flows: List[Float64]) -> Float64:
    var total: Float64 = 0.0
    for t in range(len(cash_flows)):
        total += cash_flows[t] / pow(1.0 + rate, Float64(t))   # exponent must be a Float64
    return total

def main():
    var cf = List[Float64](-1000.0, 300.0, 400.0, 500.0, 600.0)
    print("NPV = ", npv(0.10, cf))     # NPV = 388.77  -> positive, so invest

3.3 Worked example: a project that loses

The “should you invest?” case from the sources: $10,000 today for $3,000, $4,000, $5,000 over three years at the same 10%:

from std.math import pow

def calculate_npv(rate: Float64, cash_flows: List[Float64]) -> Float64:
    var npv: Float64 = 0.0
    for t in range(len(cash_flows)):
        npv += cash_flows[t] / pow(1.0 + rate, Float64(t))
    return npv

def main():
    var cf = List[Float64](-10000.0, 3000.0, 4000.0, 5000.0)
    print("Net Present Value: ", calculate_npv(0.10, cf))
    # Net Present Value: -210.37  ->  REJECT: your $10,000 grows to only
    # $9,789.63 of today's money (PV of the inflows) - you'd lose value.
Note the arithmetic fact the sources got wrong. The sources printed −38.17, but the correct value is −210.37 (3,000/1.10 + 4,000/1.21 + 5,000/1.331 = 9,789.63). The verdict is the same either way — reject — so always state the number you computed, and re-derive it by hand before publishing. It is a golden rule that a tutorial’s printed output is the least trustworthy line in the file.
Sanity hook. The inflows sum to $12,000 — nominally the project makes $2,000. Yet it loses value, because those $12,000 arrive over three years and are worth only $9,790 in today’s money. Nominal profit ≠ value created; the discount rate is the gap between the two. This is the entire lesson of NPV in one sentence.

4. The NPV Profile & IRR

NPV bends with the discount rate: raise r and future inflows shrink, so NPV falls. Plot NPV against r and you get the project’s NPV profile. The rate where the curve crosses zero is its internal rate of return (IRR) — the compound return the project actually earns on the money tied up.

Sweeping many candidate rates is a plain loop — the same npv from §3.2 evaluated at each rate, which is exactly the shape a compiled sweep wants:

def main():
    var cf = List[Float64](-1000.0, 300.0, 400.0, 500.0, 600.0)
    var rates = List[Float64](0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, 0.30)
    for i in range(len(rates)):
        print("r=", rates[i], "  NPV=", npv(rates[i], cf))     # npv() from section 3.2
    # r= 0.05   NPV=  574.07
    # r= 0.1    NPV=  388.77
    # r= 0.15   NPV=  235.14
    # r= 0.2    NPV=  106.48
    # r= 0.25   NPV=   -2.24    <- NPV crosses zero just here: IRR ~ 24.9%
    # r= 0.3    NPV=  -94.88

Interpreting the profile

  • IRR is a hurdle, not a guarantee. If your cost of capital is 10%, this project’s 24.9% IRR beats it with room to spare — accept. If the cost of capital were 26%+, NPV would be negative — reject. IRR and the NPV>0 rule agree at every point on the curve: they are the same decision drawn two ways.
  • Where they disagree. IRR can be misleading for non-conventional flows (a sign change mid-stream can give two IRRs; comparing two projects on IRR alone ignores scale and timing). When in doubt, decide on NPV — it is the defensible number.
  • Mojo. The sweep is a plain loop over pow(1.0 + r, Float64(t)) — identical shape to the NPV loop above, compiled to native speed. For thousands of candidate projects (a real portfolio screen), that is where compiled code earns its keep.

5. Loan Amortisation

A fixed-payment loan keeps the same payment every month, yet the split inside never stops changing: each payment first settles the interest on the current balance, and whatever is left chips away principal. Early payments are mostly interest; late payments are mostly principal. That decay is 30 years of your mortgage in miniature.

5.1 The level-payment formula

For principal P, n monthly periods at monthly rate r = annual/12, the level payment M is the annuity formula — the two equivalent ways you will see it written:

M = P · [ r(1+r)n / ((1+r)n − 1) ]   ≡   P · r / (1 − (1+r)−n)

The trick for reading the second form: “the payment such that the present value of the entire payment series equals the principal you walk away with.” That makes amortisation the inverse of the NPV idea from Section 3 — same equation, solved for a different unknown.

5.2 Worked examples

Example A — a $100,000 machine at 6% annual over 30 years (from the quick tutorial). First the payment, then a full month-by-month schedule:

Mojo 1.x — carrying each row in a struct is exactly what a compiled scheduler would emit (the Applications-track lesson *Amortisation Schedules* does the same):

from std.math import pow

struct AmortRow:
    var period: Int
    var payment: Float64
    var interest: Float64
    var principal: Float64
    var balance: Float64

def amortization_schedule(principal: Float64, annual_rate: Float64, years: Int) -> List[AmortRow]:
    var r = annual_rate / 12.0
    var n = years * 12
    var payment = principal * (r * pow(1.0 + r, Float64(n))) / (pow(1.0 + r, Float64(n)) - 1.0)
    var balance = principal
    var schedule = List[AmortRow]()
    for month in range(1, n + 1):
        var interest = balance * r              # interest first...
        var principal_paid = payment - interest
        balance -= principal_paid               # ...remainder pays down principal
        schedule.push_back(AmortRow(month, payment, interest, principal_paid, balance))
    return schedule

def main():
    var schedule = amortization_schedule(100000.0, 0.06, 30)
    var payment = schedule[0].payment
    print("Monthly payment = ", payment)                      # 599.55
    print("Total interest  = ", payment * 360.0 - 100000.0)   # 115838.19
    var shown = List[Int](0, 1, 2, 359)      # first three months, then the last
    for i in shown:
        var row = schedule[i]
        print("Month ", row.period, ": Pay ", row.payment, "  Int ", row.interest,
              "  Prin ", row.principal, "  Bal ", row.balance)
    # Month 1: Pay 599.55  Int 500.00  Prin 99.55   Bal 99900.45
    # Month 2: Pay 599.55  Int 499.50  Prin 100.05  Bal 99800.40
    # Month 3: Pay 599.55  Int 499.00  Prin 100.55  Bal 99699.85
    # Month 360: Pay 599.55  Int 2.98  Prin 596.57  Bal 0.00

Example B — a $250,000 mortgage at 4.5% over 30 years (from the financial-math tutorial):

from std.math import pow

def calculate_amortisation(principal: Float64, annual_rate: Float64, years: Int) -> (Float64, Float64):
    var r = annual_rate / 12.0
    var n = Float64(years * 12)
    var pmt = principal * (r * pow(1.0 + r, n)) / (pow(1.0 + r, n) - 1.0)
    var total_interest = pmt * n - principal
    return pmt, total_interest

def main():
    var pmt: Float64
    var interest: Float64
    pmt, interest = calculate_amortisation(250000.0, 0.045, 30)
    print("Monthly Payment: ", pmt)          # 1266.71
    print("Total Interest:  ", interest)      # 206016.78

5.3 Anatomy of the schedule

Notice the pattern in the four rows above — interest falls (500 → 2.98), principal rises (99.55 → 596.57), the payment never moves. The crossover month, where principal finally exceeds interest, is well into the loan’s life — month 223 here (year 18½) — and the balance crawls: after 60 payments you still owe 93,054.36 of a 100,000 principal, with 29,027.39 of interest already paid.

RowPaymentInterestPrincipalBalance after
Month 1599.55500.00 (83%)99.5599,900.45
Month 2599.55499.50100.0599,800.40
Month 60599.55465.27134.2893,054.36

Why the balance crawls early. Five years in, you have paid 60 × $599.55 ≈ $35,973 — yet owe $93,054 of the original $100,000. Roughly $29,027 of those payments were interest and only ~$7,000 reduced principal. Mortgages feel slow for the first decade because interest always lands on the (still huge) outstanding balance first.

6. Paying Down Faster

Because interest is charged on the outstanding balance, extra principal today cuts every future interest bill. Two realistic strategies on the $100,000 @ 6% / 30-year loan:

StrategyMonthly planLoan paid offTotal interestSaved vs plan
Baseline$599.55 / mo30 years (360 mo)$115,838.19—
Extra $100 / mo$699.55 / mo21 years (252 mo)$75,937.94$39,900.25
Lump $10,000$599.55, +$10,000 at month 60~25 years (297 mo)$87,788.40$28,049.79
def how_long(principal: Float64, annual_rate: Float64, years: Int, extra: Float64, extra_at: Int = -1):
    var r = annual_rate / 12.0
    var n = years * 12
    var pmt_base = principal * (r * pow(1.0 + r, Float64(n))) / (pow(1.0 + r, Float64(n)) - 1.0)
    var balance: Float64 = principal
    var months: Int = 0
    var interest_total: Float64 = 0.0
    while balance > 0 and months < n * 2:
        months += 1
        if extra_at > 0 and months == extra_at:
            balance -= 10000.0
        var cur_payment = pmt_base + extra
        interest_total += balance * r
        balance -= (cur_payment) - balance * r
        if balance < 0:
            balance = 0
    return months, interest_total

def main():
    print(how_long(100000.0, 0.06, 30, extra=0.0))                # (360, 115838.19)
    print(how_long(100000.0, 0.06, 30, extra=100.0))              # (252,  75937.94)
    print(how_long(100000.0, 0.06, 30, extra=0.0, extra_at=61))   # (297, 87788.40)
Rule of thumb. A small, constant extra payment is the single highest-return "investment" most borrowers own: every dollar of extra principal avoids ~2 dollars of interest at a 6% / 30-year rate — a guaranteed, tax-free return. The same loop in Mojo is trivial — pure Float64 arithmetic, compiled — and scales to sweeping thousands of candidate plans.

7. Putting It Together: a Loan-Funded Project

The sources close with a classic: borrow $50,000 to start a project, repay over 5 years at 5% annual interest, and expect $15,000 of inflows a year. Is it worth it? We now have every piece — the loan gives the monthly payment, and NPV decides whether the net inflows cover it with value to spare.

Three moves: (1) the loan determines the monthly debt service; (2) netting it against the expected inflows gives the annual cash the project actually delivers; (3) discounting that net stream at the firm’s cost of capital is the project’s NPV. The loan itself is funding, not an outflow — the discount rate already prices the cost of the money.

from std.math import pow

def monthly_payment(principal: Float64, annual_rate: Float64, years: Int) -> Float64:
    var r = annual_rate / 12.0
    var n = Float64(years * 12)
    return principal * (r * pow(1.0 + r, n)) / (pow(1.0 + r, n) - 1.0)

def npv_discrete(cash_flows: List[Float64], rate: Float64) -> Float64:
    var total: Float64 = 0.0
    for t in range(len(cash_flows)):
        total += cash_flows[t] / pow(1.0 + rate, Float64(t))
    return total

def main():
    var LOAN: Float64 = 50000.0
    var APY: Float64 = 0.05
    var YEARS: Int = 5
    var INFLOWS: Float64 = 15000.0
    var DISCOUNT: Float64 = 0.10

    var m = monthly_payment(LOAN, APY, YEARS)        # 943.56
    var annual_debt = 12.0 * m                       # 11322.74
    var net_annual = INFLOWS - annual_debt           # 3677.26

    print("Monthly loan payment = ", m)
    print("Annual debt service  = ", annual_debt)
    print("Net annual inflow    = ", net_annual)

    var stream = List[Float64](0.0)
    for t in range(YEARS):
        stream.push_back(net_annual)                 # 5 years of net inflows
    var project_npv = npv_discrete(stream, DISCOUNT)
    print("Project NPV @ ", DISCOUNT * 100.0, "% = ", project_npv)  # +13939.71
The qualification this model blurs. Real valuations would model the loan drawdown as a Year-0 inflow, separate equity from debt, and treat the 15,000 as the project’s operating surplus rather than raw revenue. Here the pedagogy is the point: the payment from Section 5 and the discounting from Section 3 fit together — finance is one machinery, used twice.

8. Pitfalls & Evaluation Checklist

TrapWhat goes wrongFix
Discounting CF₀Dividing today’s outflow by (1+r)⁰… no — by (1+r)1 — shrinking the cost.enumerate from 0; cash flow at t=0 is today’s money, never discounted.
Annual vs monthly rateUsing the annual rate on monthly periods (or using 6% as 0.06 when it means 6% per year).Always rm = annual/12 for monthly schedules; keep period and rate in the same unit.
Trusting printed outputThe source tutorials printed NPV = 382.07 and −38.17; the true values are 388.77 and −210.37.Recompute sample numbers by hand — and in a second language — before publishing them. A tutorial’s output is its least trustworthy line.
IRR over NPVIRR misleads on non-conventional cash flows (multiple sign changes → multiple IRRs) and rewards tiny quick projects over big slow ones.Decide on NPV; report IRR only as context against the hurdle rate.
Missing the “profit illusion”Inflows summing to $12,000 on a $10,000 investment feels like +$2,000.Discount first: $12,000 of future money can be worth only ~$9,790 today. Nominal ≠ real.
Off-by-one in exponentsRaising to t where t counts from 1, or using t-1 on the first inflow.Walk through PV of CF₁ by hand once: CF₁/(1+r)¹ must equal the obvious amount.
Forgetting amortisation’s interest-first splitTreating each payment as equal principal.Interest = balance × r first; the remainder is principal. That is the whole schedule, and Dr. Excel agrees.

Evaluation checklist

  • Sign convention: outflow negative, inflow positive; NPV sign matches the verdict.
  • Units: every rate, period, and flow share one time unit — check the monthly rm before trusting a payment.
  • Round-trip: re-derive one number in clear text, e.g. PV(CF₁) = CF₁/(1+r) — if the code disagrees, the code is wrong.
  • Cross-check the arithmetic: reproduce the same schedule with a published loan calculator or a spreadsheet; the payment and total interest must agree to cents (mind floating-point on the final row).
  • State the decision: “NPV = +388.77, so under the 10% cost of capital the project creates value” — not just a formula dump.

9. Practice & Self-Check

  1. By hand. A project costs $2,000 today and returns $1,100 and $1,210 in years 1 and 2. Compute the NPV at 10% exactly (answers: 1,100/1.1 = 1,000 exactly; 1,210/1.21 = 1,000 exactly; NPV = 0). What decision does the rule give, and what does that NPV = 0 state about the project? (It just earns its 10% cost of capital — indifferent.)
  2. Decision. Two candidates — A: −1,000 then [300, 400, 500, 600] (Section 3.2); B: −10,000 then [3,000, 4,000, 5,000] (Section 3.3). At a 10% cost of capital each is accept / reject — justify each with NPV, not gut feel.
  3. Profile. For candidate A estimate the IRR from the Section 4 profile table. What happens to the decision if your cost of capital is 26%? (NPV turns negative — accept only when the hurdle is below the IRR.)
  4. Schedule. A $120,000 loan at 4.8% APR, 15 years. Write the Mojo from Section 5.2; report the payment, the total interest, and the Row-1 / Row-180 split. Now add $200/month — how many months does the loan take, and how much interest does it save? (The Section 6 machinery answers it.)
  5. Mojo port. Port exercise 4’s function to Mojo: a struct AmortRow scheduler plus a payment-only helper via pow. Confirm both languages agree to the cent (last-row floating-point fuzz aside).
  6. Bridge to the sources. Reproduce the Section 7 loan-funded project in your own numbers: $80,000 borrowed at 4% for 10 years, $9,000 of annual inflows, 12% cost of capital. Is the NPV positive? What single variable turns it negative?
