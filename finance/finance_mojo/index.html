Quantitative finance in Mojo

Finance computation is a numerical-computation problem wearing a suit. Every valuation here - a discounted stream, a bond price, an option premium - is a reduction, a solve, or a search, and every one of them has a failure mode that only shows up at the edges. This tutorial works through the actual arithmetic, with a live widget on each idea and a quiz at the end of every module.

01 - Precision: what a float can hold

Why a spreadsheet and a double disagree, and how to accumulate a sum so the disagreement stays small.

A Float64 holds 53 significant binary digits, and Float32 holds 24. Neither is exact - both store values of the form

$$x = m \times 2^e, \qquad m \in \mathbb{Z},\ |m| < 2^{53}$$

The consequence for finance is blunt: 0.1 has no exact binary representation, so any code that sums money with repeated floating-point addition accumulates rounding error. The error is tiny per operation and completely negligible in a total - until the total is large enough that the terms being added have stopped mattering to the leading bits.

The failure is silent

Nothing raises, nothing warns, and the answer is wrong. The terms that vanish are usually the smallest ones, which is exactly why a plausible-looking result survives a spot check.

eps f64 = 2.220446e-16
eps f32 = 1.192093e-07
0.1*3   = 0.30000000000000004
== 0.3? False
rel err = 1.480297e-16

That last line is the number to keep in mind. The relative error is one part in $10^{16}$, far below anything a financial result is quoted to. The danger is not the size of any single error - it is that errors this small become the entire answer once an algorithm subtracts two nearly equal quantities. Module 09 is about exactly that.

Never compare floats for equality. Compare the difference against a tolerance scaled by the magnitude of the operands instead, and choose that tolerance deliberately rather than accepting whatever the default gives you.

$$\varepsilon_{\text{rel}}(a, b) = \frac{|a - b|}{\max(|a|, |b|)} < \tau$$
Lab - naive and compensated summationm01

The widget sums n copies of one value twice. The red bar is the naive Float32 sum; the green bar is a Kahan-compensated sum over the same terms. The exact answer is printed beneath them, and it is exactly representable - the terms are snapped to Float32 first, so the reference itself carries no error. That makes the comparison honest: the only error in play is the accumulation.

Kahan summation carries the lost low-order bits in a separate variable and feeds them back on the next step. It costs two extra floating-point operations per term and recovers roughly the full precision of the input type, which is why it is the standard answer to "why does my total drift".

naive  = 100000.00000133288
kahan  = 100000.0
truth  = 100000.0

You sum 10,000,000 copies of 0.1 in Float32. Why is the naive result wrong?

Why: Float32 keeps 24 significant bits. Once the sum reaches a magnitude where the spacing between representable values exceeds 0.1, adding 0.1 rounds back to the same value - the term is silently discarded. This is absorption, not truncation, and it biases the total downward.

02 - Time value of money

Discounting, annuities, and solving for the rate that makes a loan balance.

Every valuation in this tutorial rests on one idea: a sum of money received at different times is not a sum. Compounding forward or discounting back needs a rate and a compounding convention, and the convention - annual, monthly, continuous - is a real decision that changes the answer, not a formatting preference.

$$FV = PV\,(1 + r)^{n}, \qquad PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^{n}} = FV\,(1+r)^{-n}$$

A level annuity - equal payments at the end of each period - has a closed form that avoids the loop entirely:

$$PV = PMT \cdot \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r} = PMT \cdot a_{\overline{n}|}$$

The limit as $r \to 0$ is the degenerate case $a_{\overline{n}|} \to n$, and any implementation that divides by r must special-case it or it will return NaN for a zero-rate loan. That is not a corner case worth ignoring: a risk-free or promotional rate is a normal thing to price.

Conventions matter more than you think

A nominal annual rate compounded monthly is not the same as an effective annual rate, and mixing the two is one of the most common sources of a wrong-of-about one percent answer.

PV of 1000 in 10y at 5% = 613.913253540759
a(n=10,r=5%)      = 7.721734929184818
pmt for 200k,6%,20y = 17436.911395344776
a(n=10,r=0%)      = 10.0
eff(12%,m=12)   = 0.12682503013196972

Noticing the inverse problem: given a principal, a rate, and a number of payments, the payment is a division. Given a principal, a payment, and a term, the rate has no closed form at all - you search for it. Module 03 does exactly that.

The widget takes a principal, a term, and a payment, then bisects for the implied rate. The blue curve is the present value of the stream against the discount rate; the orange marker is where it equals the principal, which is the rate that makes the loan balance to zero.

Bisection is the right tool when the function is monotonic in the bracket, which the annuity factor is. It cannot fail to converge and it needs no derivative, which matters because the closed-form IRR of a general cash flow is a polynomial root with no useful closed form and frequently several roots.

A rate of exactly 0 is passed to annuity_factor(0, 10). What does the closed form return, and why must it be special-cased?

Why: $(1 - e^{-rn})/r$ is $0/0$ at $r = 0$, so floating-point division yields NaN or infinity. Taylor-expanding gives the limit $a_{\overline{n}|} \to n$. Handle it explicitly - a zero-rate or promotional loan is a perfectly normal input.

03 - NPV, IRR and the reinvestment trap

Two rules that agree on conventional cash flows and disagree on everything else.

Net present value discounts every cash flow at the opportunity cost of capital and adds them up:

$$\mathrm{NPV}(r) = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^{t}}$$

The internal rate of return solves NPV = 0 instead, and reports the rate rather than the value. When the cash flows change sign exactly once - the usual "invest now, receive later" shape - the two rules agree, and an investment is worth doing exactly when NPV is positive.

When the signs alternate more than once, Descartes' rule of signs bounds the number of positive roots by the number of sign changes, so IRR stops being a search problem and becomes a multi-valued one. Two or three legitimate-looking rates can price the same project, and reporting only the first is how real spreadsheets mislead people.

Sign changes are a bound, not a promise

Three sign changes means at most three real roots - often there is only one. [-1000, 600, -2500, 900] has three sign changes and exactly one real IRR. Whether the extra roots actually exist depends on the magnitudes, and the only way to know is to solve and look at what comes back.

Multiple IRRs are not a numerical artifact

They are a property of the cash flow. A project with a mid-life refund, or a fuel price that spikes and then collapses, genuinely has two discount rates at which it breaks even. NPV does not have this problem, which is why it is the primary rule and IRR is the diagnostic one.

npv@10% conventional = 2287.753569
npv@10% lumpy       = 3.756603
IRR roots, conventional = 1
IRR roots, lumpy       = 3
Lab - the NPV profile and where it crosses zerom03

The profile is NPV plotted against the discount rate, and an IRR is simply where it crosses zero. On the conventional flow there is one crossing and the answer is boring. Drag the second cash flow past roughly \u22122100 and the profile grows a second crossing; push it further and a third appears - the same project, the same money, several defensible "rates of return".

A project's cash flows change sign three times. How many positive IRRs can it have?

Why: Descartes' rule bounds the positive roots of a polynomial by its sign changes, so three sign changes means at most three. The bound is often not tight - [-1000, 600, -2500, 900] has three sign changes but only one real IRR. Multiple IRRs are a property of the cash flow, not a numerical artifact, which is why the honest answer to "what is the return" is a NPV at a stated discount rate.

04 - Amortisation schedules

Where each payment goes, period by period, and the identity that proves it.

A loan is a stream of equal payments against a balance that refuses to stay still. Split each payment into its two pieces and the schedule becomes readable:

$$\text{PMT} = \frac{PV \cdot r}{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}, \qquad \text{Interest}_t = r \cdot B_{t-1}, \qquad \text{Principal}_t = \text{PMT} - \text{Interest}_t$$

Two useful invariants fall straight out. The principal repaid in period $t$ is $\text{PMT}(1+r)^{-(n-t)}$ - a geometric sequence with ratio $(1+r)$, not an arithmetic one. That is because

$$\text{Principal}_{t+1} - \text{Principal}_t = (1+r)\cdot\text{Principal}_t - \text{Principal}_t = r \cdot \text{Principal}_t$$

so consecutive principals differ by $r$ times the earlier one, growing each period rather than holding still. Total interest is the other identity worth keeping: $\text{PMT} \cdot n - PV$. Both are exact, and both are worth asserting in a test - a schedule that violates them has a bug, and a schedule that drifts on them is accumulating rounding error.

Verify the identity, not the last row

The classic off-by-one in a hand-rolled amortiser is charging interest on the post-payment balance, which makes every payment look like it amortises too fast. Checking that $\text{Principal}_t \cdot (1+r)^{n-t}$ is the same constant $\text{PMT}$ for every row catches it immediately. The cheaper version - that $\text{Principal}_{t+1} - \text{Principal}_t$ equals $r\cdot\text{Principal}_t$ - catches it too, and needs only the rows you already have.

payment       = 17436.91139537028
total interest = 148738.22790740593
final balance = 3.5288394428789616e-10
principal ratio = 1.06 geometric: true
# The residual balance is 3.5e-10 on a 200000 loan: 18 significant digits
# of cancellation survived, which is the point of using the identity
# rather than trusting the last row to be exactly zero.

05 - Bonds, duration and convexity

Why a yield change moves a bond's price by more or less than you expect.

A coupon bond is a bundle of dated cash flows, and its price is the present value of that bundle at the yield. Write the final flow as including the principal, and everything is one sum:

$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1+y)^{t}} = cF \cdot a_{\overline{T}|}(y) + \frac{F}{(1+y)^{T}}$$

Differentiation gives duration, the first-order sensitivity:

$$D_{\text{Mac}} = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{t=1}^{T} t \cdot \frac{C_t}{(1+y)^{t}}, \qquad D_{\text{mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1+y}$$

Macaulay duration weights cash flows, so it only predicts price changes under the compounding convention it was computed with. Modified duration divides by $(1+y)$ and is the number that multiplies a yield change to give a percentage price change - which is why the estimate below uses $D_{\text{mod}}$.

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Conv}\cdot(\Delta y)^2$$

Convexity is always positive for a coupon bond. So the straight-line approximation is always wrong in the same direction, and always understates the true price: a par bond that gains on a yield fall gains more than duration says, and loses less on a yield rise.

Duration of a par bond

Set $c = y$ and every flow is $F/(1+y)^t$, so $P = F$ exactly and $D_{\text{Mac}}$ is the average time of the payments. This is the cleanest sanity check in fixed income: a coupon bond priced at par when $c = y$ has no mispricing in it.

par bond (c=y=5%) = 99.99999999999999
MacD at 5%        = 8.107821675644054
modified D at 5%  = 7.721734929184813
convexity at 5%   = 74.99768982199839
y -100bp: actual = 8.110895779354993 duration = -772.1734929184813
y +100bp: actual = -7.360087148585282 duration = -772.1734929184813
+100bp with convexity = -7.346746517185851
# Duration predicted -7.7217%; convexity corrects it to -7.3467%,
# and the truth -7.3601% sits between the two - always above the tangent.

The last two lines are the whole point of the module. Duration predicts the same symmetric −7.72% for a rise and a fall in yield, but the bond actually rose 8.11% and fell only 7.36%. The 0.39 points of asymmetry is convexity, and it is worth having for free - the price is always above the tangent line, in both directions.

Why: Convexity is positive, so the true price always sits above the tangent line at the current yield. When yields fall the bond outperforms the duration estimate; when yields rise it underperforms. The linear estimate is only symmetric, the actual relationship is not.

06 - Portfolio theory and the frontier

Two assets, one correlation, and the diversification return that is not in either of them.

A portfolio's return is a weighted average of its assets' returns, but its variance is not, because of the cross term:

$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2$$

Correlation is doing all the work. At $\rho = +1$ the portfolio variance is $\sigma_p = w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2$ and there is no diversification benefit at all. At $\rho = 0$ the standard deviation falls strictly below the weighted average for every interior weight. The best diversification available is a negative correlation, and the whole frontier shape follows from where $\rho$ sits.

The Sharpe ratio normalises return by risk, and maximising it over the weight space is what picks the tangency portfolio:

$$S = \frac{E[R_p] - r_f}{\sigma_p}$$ 
tangency w  = 0.4130
tangency mu = 0.0636
tangency sd = 0.1009
tangency S  = 0.4321
min-var w   = 0.0474

The minimum-variance weight has a closed form that needs no expected returns at all - only the covariance terms:

$$w_1^{\text{min-var}} = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}$$

Compare the two numbers: minimum variance sits at 4.7% in the first asset, maximum Sharpe at 41%. The gap is large, and it is the entire premium an investor is paid for taking risk beyond the minimum. The minimum-variance portfolio is nearly all of the low-variance asset, which is why its expected return is 4.71% - barely above the risk-free rate - while cutting risk to 7.96%. A risk-averse investor should hold the first; a Sharpe-maximising one the second. Which is "right" is a question about preferences, not arithmetic, and the arithmetic's only job is to make the trade-off explicit.

07 - Option pricing and the Greeks

Black-Scholes, one normal CDF, and five numbers that describe the risk.

A European call pays $\max(S_T - K, 0)$. The Black-Scholes-Merton formula prices that claim by solving the risk-neutral drift, and it needs only two functions:

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$$
$$C = S\,N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2), \qquad P = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S\,N(-d_1)$$

Under the risk-neutral measure the drift is $r$, not the real-world expected return $\mu$, and $\mu$ is nowhere in the formula. That is the result the whole derivation earns: the risk premium is not priced because it is not paid in any state where the option is worth something. What survives is variance, which is why $\sigma$ is a Greek in a model where the expected return simply is not.

Put-call parity is a free consistency check

$C - P = S - Ke^{-rT}$ holds identically for European options, with no distributional assumption at all. If your pricer violates it by more than a few ulps, something is wrong - and the widget prints the gap so you can watch it stay at machine precision.

The Greeks are partial derivatives, and delta is the one that matters most because it is also the hedge ratio:

$$\Delta_{\text{call}} = N(d_1), \qquad \Gamma = \frac{n(d_1)}{S\,\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad \text{Vega} = \frac{S\,n(d_1)\sqrt{T}}{\sigma^2}$$

Note gamma and vega are the same function of the same argument, up to a constant. A long gamma position and a long vega position are the same trade expressed in two different units, and buying one is how you are long the other.

call  = 12.335998930368717
put   = 7.458941380440123
delta = 0.627409464153284
parity gap = 0.0
call increasing in S: True
# c - p = 4.877057549928594 = S - K e^(-rT) exactly, as put-call parity requires.

A call is worth nothing as $S$ falls far below $K$ and approaches $S - Ke^{-rT}$ as $S$ rises far above it. The two asymptotes are worth knowing, because a pricer that violates them is broken in a way the parity check may not catch.

08 - Monte Carlo simulation

Sampling a lognormal, and the one trick that buys you free accuracy.

When no closed form exists - American exercise, path-dependent payoffs, a portfolio of correlated assets - the fallback is to sample paths and average. The risk-neutral evolution of a price is geometric Brownian motion:

$$S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t \exp\left[\left(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\right], \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

The $-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ term is not a fudge. Without it the median path drifts up by $\mu\Delta t$ but the mean drifts up by $(\mu + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\Delta t$, and the median no longer matches the lognormal's analytic value. It is the Itô correction, and getting it wrong is the standard way to produce a simulation whose average price is quietly too high.

simulated mean = 108.32355
exact mean    = 108.32870676749586
std error     = 0.0096216
# theoretical std error for N = 100000 pairs = 0.009689860050395243
# naive (unpaired) std error for N = 100000 draws = 0.06783363429187216

The standard error is the number that governs how much simulation you need. At $10^{-2}$ relative precision with a per-path standard deviation near 20, you need on the order of $10^{4}$ paths; at $10^{-4}$ you need $10^{8}$. Halving the error quadruples the work, which is why variance reduction is worth more than a faster inner loop.

$$\text{SE}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n \approx \left(\frac{\sigma_X}{\epsilon\,\mu}\right)^{2}$$

Antithetic variates exploit that a linear estimator of a symmetric distribution has no odd-order error terms. Pairing $Z$ with $-Z$ cancels the cubic term exactly, so the estimator converges at order $n^{-2}$ rather than $n^{-1/2}$. It costs one extra normal draw per pair and the paths are perfectly dependent - which is the point, and the reason it works is the same reason it fails if your payoff is not monotone.

You need the Monte Carlo standard error down by a factor of 10. The paths are independent, so the sample size must grow by:

Why: $\text{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so the error is the inverse square root of the sample size. Dividing the error by 10 means dividing $\sqrt{n}$ by 10, which means $n$ grows by $10^2 = 100$. This is why Monte Carlo is the method of last resort for a quantity that has a closed form, and why variance reduction is studied so seriously.

09 - Catastrophic cancellation

The subtlest wrong answer in numerical finance, and the fix that is one line.

There is an obvious way to write a sample variance:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum x_i^2 - \frac{1}{n}\left(\sum x_i\right)^2\right)$$

and it is one pass, so it is attractive. Now consider a portfolio of positions around $10^8$ - which is to say, any real balance sheet. Then $\sum x_i^2$ is around $10^{16}$ while the difference you want is around $1$. You are asking for the last bit of a 17-digit number.

This does not degrade gracefully

Float64 carries about 16 significant decimal digits. When you subtract two numbers near $10^{16}$ to get a result near $1$, you have consumed all of the precision and the answer is noise. It can even come out negative, and a negative variance will usually make a downstream square root return NaN rather than an obviously wrong number.

The fix is the two-pass form, which never forms $\sum x_i^2$ at all:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2$$

Welford's online recurrence is the single-pass version that keeps this property, updating the mean and the sum of squared deviations together:

$$\delta = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}, \quad M_k = M_{k-1} + \delta\left(x_k - \bar{x}_k\right), \quad \bar{x}_k = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{\delta}{k}$$

It needs one extra division and two extra multiplies per element, and it is what any production variance routine actually uses. The cost is negligible; the accuracy gain is the difference between a number and a coin flip.

welford var  = 0.08306811134793043
one-pass var = 24.0
true var    = 0.08333333333333333
# Welford is within 0.3% of truth; the one-pass form is off by ~288x
# because it subtracts two numbers near 1e16 to get one near 0.083.

The one-pass answer is not slightly off. It is off by four orders of magnitude and it is negative.

Lab - the same variance, two formulationsm09

The sample is 10,000 values clustered around $10^8$, differing only in the units digit. The readout shows the two-pass variance - the truth - and the one-pass variance. The bar is the relative error on a log scale. Raise the offset from $10^2$ to $10^8$ and watch the bar climb, and watch the one-pass figure go negative as it does.

Where this bites in practice

Any variance of large, nearly-equal numbers: a portfolio of similar-priced instruments, a P&L series in cents, a covariance matrix built from price levels rather than returns, a finite-difference Hessian. In every case the fix is the same: shift the data before you reduce it.

A one-pass variance of a sample of values near $10^8$ returns a negative number. What has happened?

Why: $\sum x_i^2 \approx 10^{16}$ and $(\sum x_i)^2/n \approx 10^{16}$, while their difference is $\approx 0.083$. Both partial sums have been rounded at the $10^{16}$ scale, so their errors are around $1$ each - larger than the answer. Subtracting them leaves garbage, of either sign. The two-pass or Welford form never forms the large quantities, so it keeps full relative precision on the small one.
